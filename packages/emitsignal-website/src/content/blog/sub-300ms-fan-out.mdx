When you `curl` a message into a topic, the easy part is accepting it. The hard part is everything after the 200 OK: that one signal might need to reach a phone via APNs, three terminals over a websocket, a Slack channel, two webhooks, and an email digest — and a human is watching at least one of those, tapping their foot.

For most of last year that fan-out had a p99 of 1.4 seconds. That is fine for a deploy notice and completely unacceptable for a page. This is the story of getting it to 280ms.

## The old path

The original design was the obvious one: accept the publish, write it to Postgres, and let a worker pool pick it up and deliver to each subscriber. Durable, simple, and slow — every delivery waited behind a database round-trip and a queue hop.

```text
publish → http api → postgres (insert)
                  → notify worker
   worker poll  → fetch subscribers
                → deliver one at a time
   p99: ~1,400ms
```

<Callout tone="amber">
    The queue was not the bottleneck. The fan-out being serial was. We delivered to subscribers in a
    loop, awaiting each sink.
</Callout>

## Fan-out as a first-class step

We split delivery into two planes. The control plane still writes durably to Postgres so nothing is ever lost. The data plane fans out in parallel from an in-memory subscriber index, keyed by topic, kept warm on every node.

```rust
// deliver to every matching sink, concurrently
let sinks = index.match(&topic, &signal);
let delivered = futures::stream::iter(sinks)
    .map(|sink| sink.deliver(&signal))
    .buffer_unordered(256)   // <- the whole trick
    .collect::<Vec<_>>()
    .await;

metrics::record_fanout(topic, delivered.len());
```

`buffer_unordered(256)` means a topic with 200 subscribers delivers to all of them at once, not one after another. The slowest sink sets the latency, not the sum of all of them.

## Keeping the index warm

An in-memory index is only fast if it is actually in memory on the node taking the request. We replicate subscription changes over the same websocket layer we use for delivery — a subscription is just another signal on an internal topic.

- Subscribe / unsubscribe publishes to `_sys/subs`.
- Every node listens and patches its local index.
- Cold start replays the last snapshot, then tails the log.

## What we broke

Parallelism is a great way to turn one slow sink into a thundering herd. The first version happily opened 4,000 concurrent APNs connections during a traffic spike and got us rate-limited into next week.

<Quote cite="Maya, in the postmortem">
    Concurrency without a ceiling is just an outage you scheduled for your busiest minute.
</Quote>

The `buffer_unordered` cap plus a per-sink token bucket fixed it. Now a hot topic degrades gracefully — deliveries queue per sink instead of stampeding the provider.

## Where we landed

```text
p50   41ms
p95  190ms
p99  280ms   (was 1,400ms)
max  prod-cap at 2s, then async retry
```

Sub-300ms from `curl` to lock screen, for the 99th percentile, including the slow sinks. The next target is the long tail of mobile push providers — but that is a different post.
